# Assessment & Reflection in CBL

## Rubric-Based Assessment

- CBL implementation in higher education requires assessment tools focused on **the learning process and skills acquired**, not just the final artifact — rubrics guide students during the work *and* help instructors evaluate both work-in-progress and results.
- The official CBL Rubric is meant as a **starting point** to build a personalized assessment resource: teams use it to gauge their own progress and adjust course, and teachers use it to evaluate outcomes.
- Practically: rubric criteria in CBL implementations tend to span both **content mastery** (did they actually learn the subject matter needed) and **process/competency** dimensions (collaboration, research quality, iteration, communication) — competency-based rubrics are a recurring theme in CBL assessment literature.
- Rubrics should be introduced **at the beginning** of the project, not just at grading time, so teams can use them as a working reference (e.g., "gauge their progress and make adjustments").

## Assessment Methods Used Across CBL Implementations

- Papers / written reports
- Quizzes and examinations (for content knowledge components)
- Journals
- Peer reviews
- Teacher observations
- Student–teacher conferences
- Interim work reviews, tied back to the rubric
- Completion reports at the end of the Act phase

Both **formative** (ongoing, during the process) and **summative** (final) assessment should be used — CBL treats the process itself as gradable, not just the end product.

## Reflection Practices

- Reflection is treated as a **core learning mechanism**, not an add-on: "much of the deepest learning takes place by considering the process and analyzing ongoing relationships with content and between concepts."
- Reflection should happen **throughout** the Challenge, not only at the end.
- Common reflection formats: blogs, video diaries, podcasts, digital storytelling, photo journals.
- Effective reflection activities have students explicitly discuss:
  - What they learned
  - What challenges/obstacles they hit
  - How they overcame (or didn't overcome) those obstacles
  - What they'd do differently

## Documentation as Infrastructure

- Learners document their experience continuously using audio, video, images, and photography throughout the Challenge (all three phases).
- This running documentation serves **three purposes at once**:
  1. Raw material for reflection
  2. Evidence for formative/informal assessment
  3. Content for the final "share" step (public presentation, case study, etc.)
- Because documentation feeds both reflection and assessment, it should be built into the workflow from day one (e.g., a shared log/journal), not reconstructed retroactively at the end.

## Implication for a Claude Skill

A CBL-based skill for product-building should:
- Prompt for a lightweight rubric or success-criteria definition early (during Engage or Investigate), covering both content/technical learning and process/collaboration quality.
- Nudge periodic reflection check-ins at natural transition points (end of Engage, end of Investigate, after each prototype iteration, after launch) rather than only a final retro.
- Encourage keeping a running documentation log (decisions, findings, media) that can be assembled into a final share-out artifact with minimal extra work.
