# Collaborating Using CBL

CBL is inherently a group format — its open-ended nature means teams need explicit structure to stay coordinated and avoid chaos.

## Why Structure Matters

The open framework and group-work format of CBL make it important to create structure for participants by ensuring everyone understands the roles they can play. The specific roles needed depend on the nature of the Challenge and the personalities/skills of the team. Students can (and probably should) rotate through multiple roles over the course of a project, and more than one person can share a role.

## Example Team Roles (from challengebasedlearning.org)

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| **Project Manager** | Owns the overall process: tracks progress toward deadlines, team productivity, and team morale. |
| **Documentarian** | Builds the structure/strategy for documenting the entire CBL experience (text, audio, video); coordinates with the production team to capture key events. |
| **Media Specialist** | Manages production of captured media — capture, edit, organize, distribute. |
| **Product Manager** | Owns final deliverables: presentations, print materials, web products, videos, etc. |
| **Research Manager** | Manages the Guiding Questions and the research process; collects/organizes findings from researchers; coordinates directed learning experiences and guest experts. |
| **Researcher** | Gathers resources and activities to answer Guiding Questions; assists with documentation. |
| **Public Relations / Marketing Director** | Keeps stakeholders (school, home, community) informed; handles inquiries; creates marketing materials for the solution. |
| **Social Media / Collaboration Director** | Manages private/public online spaces for the project; ensures collaboration and documentation tools are available and used well. |

These roles are not prescriptive — they should be adapted to the challenge and the team.

## Benefits of Assigned Roles

- Higher-quality, more focused interactions between team members
- People stay on task better when roles are clear and distinct
- Increases engagement and group cohesion
- Promotes individual accountability within a collaborative structure
- Lets students develop skills outside their usual strengths (rotating roles)

## The Facilitator's Job (Teacher / Mentor / Coach)

Good facilitation in CBL (and collaborative learning generally) means:

- **Enabling free discourse** — creating space for open discussion and knowledge sharing rather than lecturing
- **Scaffolding, not solving** — providing just enough structure/support to develop students' own knowledge and problem-solving skills
- **Ceding ownership** — letting learners genuinely own their challenge, questions, and solution rather than steering them to a predetermined answer
- **Challenging assumptions** — pushing learners to consider alternative perspectives and defend their reasoning
- **Structuring emergence of roles** — group structure, how members interact, and how the facilitator intervenes together shape how roles and responsibility naturally emerge in the team

## Practical Collaboration Mechanics

- Maintain a shared online space (docs, boards, chat) for both **collaboration** and **documentation** — CBL treats these as the same infrastructure, not separate tools.
- Use the Guiding Questions list as the shared backlog/roadmap during Investigate — it doubles as a task-tracking structure.
- Revisit roles and workload periodically; CBL is iterative, and role needs shift between Engage, Investigate, and Act (e.g., the Product Manager role becomes central in Act).
- Build in regular check-ins tied to the "team morale" dimension of the Project Manager role — CBL explicitly names morale as something to actively track, not just deadlines.

## Implication for a Product-Building Skill

When guiding a student team through CBL to build a product, the skill should:
1. Prompt the team to explicitly assign/rotate roles early (at least Project Manager, Researcher, and a Product/Design-focused role).
2. Keep a running shared "Guiding Questions" backlog visible throughout Investigate.
3. Encourage a lightweight facilitator posture — ask questions that build ownership rather than handing over finished answers.
